The Pilot Model: Why the Value of AI Is Inferential and Its Risk Is Operational

Joshua Idele

Independent Researcher

codewithjoshh@gmail.com

GitHub: Coding-With-Josh    X: @josh_scriptz

Preprint v3, October 2026

Abstract

Public  and  technical  debate  about  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  often  sorts  systems  into  "safe"  and
"dangerous." We argue that this framing misplaces the locus of risk and of value. Contemporary AI
systems, including large language models (LLMs) and agentic tools, produce inferences:
probabilistic  outputs  conditioned  on  training  data,  context,  and  instructions.  Their  benefit  is
inferential,  arising  when  a  human  supplies  intent  and  context  and  then  evaluates  what  is  returned.
We propose the Pilot Model, in which the user is the pilot, the AI is an instrument, and safety is a
property of the whole pilot-instrument-environment system rather than of the instrument alone. We
give  a  simple  expected-harm  decomposition  in  which  the  operator  controls  three  levers:  the
probability of error, the probability that an error is detected, and the impact of an undetected error.
We distinguish four failure modes (context starvation, the oracle fallacy, unverified autonomy, and
upstream misalignment), instantiate the model in software engineering as a validation architecture,
show how it transfers to other domains, and propose an empirical protocol to test it. We also address
the  strongest  objections,  including  that  some  systems  are  harmful  by  design,  state  where  operator
control runs out (harm without malicious intent, harm to third parties), and compare the model with
existing safety approaches. Our claim is deliberately scoped: danger is not an intrinsic property of
inference, but of deployments in which inference is given authority without verification, or is built
for harmful ends.

Keywords: AI inference; human-AI collaboration; human oversight; automation bias; verification;
software engineering

1. Introduction

When an AI system produces a wrong or harmful result, the common verdict is that the AI was "bad." This
verdict  attributes  competence  and  intent  to  a  system  that  has  neither.  A  language  model  computes  a
probability  distribution  over  possible  continuations  and  samples  from  it;  what  it  returns  is  an  inference
whose  quality  depends  on  the  evidence,  framing,  and  checking  around  it.  Safety  research  has  long
recognized that accidents in complex systems rarely have a single component as their cause [1, 2]. We apply
this view to modern AI.

This paper makes five contributions:

• A thesis. The value of AI is inferential: it generates plausible candidates (code, text, plans, hypotheses)
quickly, but it is not an authority on truth.

• A  model.  The  Pilot  Model,  with  an  expected-harm  decomposition  that  identifies  what  an  operator
controls (Section 3).

• A taxonomy. Four failure modes in the human-AI loop (Section 4).

• Limits and a comparison. Where operator control runs out, including harm without malicious intent,

• An  instantiation  and  a  test.  A  validation  architecture  for  software  engineering,  its  transfer  to  other
domains, and a proposed evaluation protocol (Sections 7-9).

We  scope  the  thesis  carefully.  We  do  not  claim  that  no  AI  system  can  contribute  to  harm.  We  claim  that
danger  is  not  an  intrinsic  property  of  inference;  it  is  a  property  of  how  systems  are  built,  incentivized,
deployed, and supervised. The productive question therefore shifts from "Is this AI dangerous?" to "Who is
piloting, with what instruments, checks, and limits on authority?"

Note on terminology. On September 29, 2026, a U.S. executive order directed federal agencies to replace
"artificial intelligence" with "super intelligence" (SI) in executive-branch communications [16]. This paper
retains  "artificial  intelligence"  and  "AI"  for  two  reasons.  It  is  the  term  used  across  the  literature  and
standards this work builds on, and "superintelligence" conventionally denotes intellect that surpasses human
capability  [17],  whereas  this  paper  concerns  systems  that  perform  fallible,  probabilistic  inference.  That
distinction is central to the argument.

2. Background and Related Work

2.1 Code generation and evaluation

Chen  et  al.  introduced  HumanEval  and  the  Codex  models,  reporting  that  functional  correctness  improves
substantially  with  repeated  sampling  and  that  automated  testing  is  necessary  to  separate  correct  from
incorrect  candidates  [3].  SWE-bench  moved  evaluation  to  real  repository  issues  and  showed  that  realistic
software  tasks  remain  difficult  without  strong  scaffolding  [4].  These  results  frame  generated  code  as
candidates that must be checked.

2.2 Security of generated output

Pearce  et  al.  found  that  a  substantial  fraction  of  Copilot-generated  programs  in  security-relevant  scenarios
contained vulnerabilities [5]. In a user study, Perry et al. reported that participants with an AI assistant wrote
less  secure  code  than  those  without,  while  being  more  confident  in  its  security  [6].  The  second  finding  is
important for our argument: the failure is jointly produced by model output and human over-trust.

2.3 Automation, trust, and human error

The  human-factors  literature  distinguishes  appropriate  use  of  automation  from  misuse  (over-reliance)  and
disuse  (under-reliance)  [7],  and  documents  complacency  and  automation  bias  when  operators  fail  to
cross-check automated aids [8]. Reason's analysis of human error [2] and Leveson's systems-theoretic view
of  safety  [1]  treat  accidents  as  failures  of  control  across  a  socio-technical  system,  which  is  the  stance  we
adopt.

2.4 AI safety and model-level concerns

Work on AI safety identifies model-level problems such as reward misspecification, distributional shift, and
unsafe exploration [9], and scholarship on large language models highlights risks from training data, scale,
and  misuse  [10].  We  take  these  seriously.  Our  position  is  that  they  are  best  addressed  as  properties  of  the

system  that  includes  builders,  data,  incentives,  and  operators,  and  that  operator-side  verification  remains
necessary even as model-side work improves.

3.1 Inference, not authority

LLM inference is probabilistic. It can be made reproducible (fixed seed, low temperature), but
reproducibility  is  not  correctness.  Where  determinism  is  required,  it  comes  from  the  surrounding  layer:
compilers, type systems, tests, invariants, and human review. We therefore separate generation
(probabilistic, fast, broad) from validation (deterministic or accountable, slower, narrow).

3.3 An expected-harm decomposition

Consider a single AI-assisted action. Let e be the probability that the output is wrong in a way that matters, d
the probability that verification detects such an error before it takes effect, and I the impact if an error takes
effect. The expected harm of the action is

E[harm] = e × (1 − d) × I

This is an illustrative decomposition, not an empirical law. It is useful because it shows where the operator
has leverage:

• Reduce e by supplying context. Precise requirements, interfaces, constraints, and examples ground the
inference.

• Raise  d  by  verifying.  Tests,  static  analysis,  cross-checks,  and  independent  review.  Over-trust  lowers
effective d, which is the mechanism of automation bias [7, 8].

• Bound I by limiting authority. Sandboxes, least privilege, staged rollout, rollback, and human sign-off
for irreversible actions.

Model builders influence the baseline e and supply tools that make d easier to achieve (uncertainty signals,
provenance, safe defaults). A better model lowers e but does not remove the need for d and a bound on I. As
autonomy  increases,  I  tends  to  grow  because  more  actions  occur  without  a  human  between  inference  and
effect, which increases rather than reduces the importance of the other two levers.

3.4 Why a pilot

Aviation illustrates the point. Instruments and autopilots greatly increase capability and safety, yet accidents
still  occur  when  instruments  are  poorly  designed,  when  pilots  over-trust  or  misread  them,  or  when
procedures  fail.  The  lesson  is  not  that  instruments  are  dangerous  but  that  safety  is  engineered  across  the
whole system, including the pilot's authority to override. The metaphor has limits (Sections 5 and 10), but it
captures the division of labor we propose.

F1-F3 are operator-side and are addressable by practice. F4 is builder- and institution-side and is addressable

intent to the model. This is also why accountability belongs to people and organizations rather than to the
instrument.

5. Harm Without Malice: Where Operator Control Runs Out

The Pilot Model does not claim that harm requires bad intent, or that a diligent operator can always prevent
it.  AI  systems  can  produce  harmful  results  when  nobody  intends  harm,  and  the  levers  in  Section  3  have
limits. We identify five.

• Model-side  error  and  misspecification.  Models  can  produce  confident  falsehoods,  and  optimizing  a
stated  objective  can  yield  behavior  its  designers  did  not  intend  [9].  This  sets  a  baseline  e  that  the
operator does not control.

• Data-driven  bias.  Training  data  can  encode  and  reproduce  social  biases  and  other  harmful  patterns
[10]. Outputs can be harmful even when the operator acts in good faith.

• Verification  limits.  Some  outputs  cannot  be  checked  cheaply  by  a  person,  such  as  novel  scientific
claims or large volumes of automated decisions. Here d is bounded no matter how diligent the operator
is.

• Third  parties.  The  people  harmed  are  often  not  the  pilot.  A  screening  or  lending  tool  affects  people
who never chose to use it and who cannot verify, override, or contest its output.

• Speed  and  scale.  Automated  systems  can  act  faster,  and  on  more  cases,  than  oversight  can  follow,
which raises I before a human can intervene.

These cases show that the Pilot Model is necessary but not sufficient. They also sharpen the thesis. Danger is
not  a  property  of  inference  as  such,  but  it  can  be  a  property  of  a  deployed  system  whose  baseline  error,
verification limits, and reach exceed what any single operator controls. In those cases responsibility moves
to  builders,  deployers,  and  regulators,  and  the  relevant  tools  are  model-side  alignment  and  evaluation,
institutional oversight, and rules about where such systems may be used (Section 6).

We make three claims about what is different. First, the model is operator-centered: it starts from what the
person using the system can control rather than from properties of the model. Second, it separates context,
verification,  and  authority  in  one  decomposition,  so  interventions  can  be  compared  and  ablated.  Third,  it
comes with measurable proxies and a test protocol (Table 2 and Section 9). We do not claim these ideas are
individually new. The contribution is their combination and operationalization for AI-assisted work, and the
claim stands or falls on the evaluation.

• Contextual  enrichment  (lowers  e).  Provide  relevant  files,  interfaces,  constraints,  and  error  logs;
structural representations such as abstract syntax trees can further ground generation.

• Decomposed  prompting  (lowers  e,  eases  d).  Break  work  into  small,  checkable  steps  rather  than
requesting a monolithic solution.

• Automated  guardrails  (raises  d).  Unit  and  property-based  tests,  type  checking,  linters,  and  security
scanners catch statistical errors that review alone may miss.

• Scoped  autonomy  (bounds  I).  Agents  receive  least-privilege  permissions,  run  in  sandboxes,  and
require human approval for irreversible operations, with audit logs and rollback.

The  architecture  positions  the  engineer  as  an  engineer  of  context  and  verification:  the  skill  that  matters  is
less typing code and more specifying intent, constructing checks, and judging results.

Domains where verification is cheap (software, arithmetic, formal checking) can support greater delegation.
Domains  where  verification  is  expensive  or  slow  (open-ended  judgment,  novel  clinical  situations)  warrant
tighter limits on authority and more human involvement. The model thus yields a practical rule: delegation
should scale with verifiability, and authority should be bounded by the cost of an undetected error.

10. Discussion: Objections and Limitations

"Some  AI  is  dangerous  by  design."  Agreed.  A  system  built  to  deceive,  surveil,  or  harm  is  a  harmful
deployment,  and  responsibility  lies  with  its  designers  and  operators.  This  is  failure  mode  F4,  not  a
counterexample to the thesis. The thesis concerns inference as such and does not excuse any application.

"Bias and insecure patterns live in the model, not the user." Partly true, and it is why we include builders
within the system analyzed. Model-level work on data, alignment, and evaluation [9, 10] is complementary
(Sections 5 and 6). Even so, the operator cannot delegate verification to the instrument whose reliability is in
question.

"Users cannot verify everything." Correct. This is why verification must be partly automated and why tool
builders should expose uncertainty, provenance, and safe defaults. A pilot needs good instruments, and the
model assigns that obligation to designers.

"The pilot metaphor weakens as autonomy grows." This is a genuine limit. Greater autonomy raises I, so
it increases the demand for oversight mechanisms such as scoped permissions, monitoring, and interruption
capability. Where meaningful human oversight is infeasible at the required speed or scale, the model advises
against granting that authority.

Limitations. The framework is conceptual and the expected-harm decomposition is illustrative; e, d, and I
are not independent in practice (for example, over-trust couples e and d). The domain analysis in Section 8 is
a sketch requiring domain expertise. The proposed study has not yet been run, and rapid model progress may
shift which failure modes dominate.

11. Conclusion

Asking  whether  an  AI  is  safe  or  dangerous  obscures  where  outcomes  are  decided.  Inference  supplies
candidates;  people  supply  direction,  context,  and  judgment;  engineering  supplies  verification  and  limits.
Value  emerges  when  the  user  pilots  with  good  instruments  and  appropriate  checks.  Harm  emerges  when
authority  is  granted  to  inference  without  verification,  or  when  systems  are  built  for  harmful  ends.  For
practitioners,  the  implication  is  to  invest  in  context,  verification,  and  bounded  authority.  For  builders  and
